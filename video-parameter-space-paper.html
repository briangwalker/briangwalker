Video Parameter Space

A Structured Model of the Measurable Variables in Video Capture, Processing, and Assembly

Project Aperture — Technical Reference · Working Draft · Version 2026-08-30

Abstract This document defines a parameter space for video and photographic capture: a set of six component spaces (Meta, Content, Capture, Audio, Post, Editing) whose independent variables, together with a small number of cross-space bridge variables, fully determine a shot. Several quantities that resemble variables — frame count, depth of field, speed ratio, field of view, among others — are shown to be derived rather than independent, in a manner directly analogous to the SI distinction between base and derived physical quantities. Named cinematographic techniques (timelapse, slow-motion, rack focus) are formalized as loci: regions of the parameter space defined by a condition on its variables, rather than as additional dimensions. The model's Meta/Content split independently reproduces the intrinsic/extrinsic camera-parameter distinction used in computer vision, and its overall structure is related to the 7-dimensional plenoptic function of Adelson & Bergen (1991). This draft documents the current variable set, their units and domains, boundary conditions, and open structural questions.

1 Introduction

The recording technology parameter space is a structured map of the measurable choices and consequences that occur across a moving-image workflow. The model is deliberately technical: it restricts itself to quantities that can be measured or set numerically (or, where necessary, categorically), and treats named creative techniques as regions within that space rather than as additional axes of it. This keeps the technical core small and falsifiable while leaving room for an open-ended catalogue of technique as the space is explored.

Section 2 gives the structural overview and the map. Section 3 is the primary reference: every variable currently in the model, with its symbol, unit, and domain. Sections 4–6 cover the three structural mechanisms that connect and constrain those variables — bridge variables, derived quantities, and boundary/feasibility conditions. Section 7 presents two graphs produced from the model. Section 8 catalogues named techniques as loci. Sections 9–10 address terminology precision and prior art. Section 11 discusses what the model has revealed so far, Section 12 lists known gaps, and Sections 13–14 are reference material.

2 Structural Overview

The parameter space is organized into six component spaces. Five describe properties of a single shot (Meta, Content, Capture, Audio, Post); one, Editing, describes relationships across multiple shots and is the only component space whose variables are not properties of a single moment. Two confirmed bridge variables connect component spaces that would otherwise be fully independent, and a growing set of derived quantities are computed from other variables rather than chosen directly.

5 Derived Quantities

Values that resemble independent variables but are fully determined by other variables. The tell: if a value falls out once the rest of the space is fixed, it is not an axis. Directly analogous to the SI distinction between base quantities (length, time) and derived quantities (velocity, force).

QuantityFormula
Frame countn = ⌊R × t⌋
Depth of fieldDoF = f(N, focal length, focus distance)
FramingFr = f(distance, focal length, aspect ratio, sensor size)
Speed ratiov = P / R

5.1 Field of view, crop-consistent form

A naive formula (horizontal FOV fixed by sensor width and focal length, vertical FOV obtained by dividing by aspect ratio) allows a portrait crop to capture more vertical field of view than the sensor physically sees — a real error, not a rounding artifact. The corrected form holds whichever dimension is not being cropped at its native value:

native horizontal FOV = 2·atan( sensor width / (2 × focal length) )
native vertical FOV  = 2·atan( sensor height / (2 × focal length) )
if target aspect ≥ native aspect: horizontal FOV = native; vertical FOV shrinks
else: vertical FOV = native; horizontal FOV shrinks

This guarantees a crop only ever removes field of view. It is the correct model for genuine in-camera or in-post cropping; it is not the mechanism this project now uses for portrait video specifically (see Camera roll, §3.2, and Loci, §8).

6 Boundary Conditions & Feasibility

6.1 Three constraint types

  • Physical — follows from what the quantity means; will never change (e.g. exposure cannot exceed the frame interval).
  • Technological — bounded by current hardware; expected to shift over time (e.g. ISO ceiling, aperture extremes).
  • Definitional — follows from how the variable is defined, independent of physics or hardware (e.g. sequence order lives in a permutation group).

6.2 Feasibility tiers

  1. Impossible by definition — the concept itself breaks down (negative aperture, negative ISO).
  2. Theoretically coherent, not natively achievable — a valid value with no current direct-capture mechanism (continuously variable resolution mid-exposure).
  3. Achievable today — real equipment does this now (focus racking, zoom-burst, exposure ramping, speed ramping).
Worked boundary case. Photography is the point {n = 1, P = 0} in Capture space — a single frame, held open indefinitely rather than advancing. It requires no special-case rule; it falls directly out of Playback rate's domain including zero.

9 Terminology

A linear-algebra subspace must contain the zero vector and be closed under addition and scalar multiplication. Categorical variables (lens type, spectrum, pickup pattern) and bounded-positive domains (aperture, ISO) fail this test, so the structure documented here is more accurately a parameter space — a general term from physics and engineering for a space of measurable settings, requiring no vector-space axioms. The six named spaces are its component spaces; named techniques (§8) are loci, not subspaces in the linear-algebra sense. "Space" and "subspace" persist as informal working names throughout this document; this section is the precise cross-reference.

10 Related Work

Adelson & Bergen's 1991 plenoptic function models all light in a scene as a 7-dimensional function of position (x, y, z), direction (θ, Φ), wavelength, and time — closely related to this document's Content and Meta spaces, reached independently from the opposite direction (modeling light itself, rather than capture settings).

Standard computer vision divides camera parameters into intrinsic (focal length, sensor and resolution properties) and extrinsic (position and orientation) — matching this document's Meta/Content split. This division was reconstructed independently before the established terms were identified; Section 2's Meta/Content pairing is the same distinction under different names.

11 Discussion

  • Every Capture-space variable is a function of Time; "static" is the special case where that function is constant. Rack focus, zoom-burst, and exposure ramping are all instances of the same underlying behavior.
  • Several apparent variables (§5) turned out to be derived quantities. The general test — does the value fall out once the rest of the space is fixed — generalized cleanly across frame count, DoF, speed ratio, shutter angle, and now FOV.
  • Two structurally unrelated mechanisms can converge on the same visible effect: an optical rack focus and a keyframed Post-space blur look similar but live in different component spaces.
  • Camera roll (§3.2) resolved a real bug in the earlier aspect-ratio model, in which portrait crops captured more vertical field of view than the sensor could physically see. Modeling portrait video as a 90° roll of a landscape frame is both the fix and, per informal review, closer to how a meaningful fraction of real vertical video is actually produced.
  • Symbol collisions are worth auditing once a model accumulates enough variables: R (Recording rate vs. Resolution), the original σ (Blur vs. Sequence order, resolved by renaming to π), and θ/φ/ρ vs. standard aerospace ψ/θ/φ (§3.2, now resolved by adopting the standard convention) are three instances of the same failure mode appearing independently.

12 Known Gaps

  • Distribution space — codec, compression, container format, and delivery constraints. The one stage of the standard production pipeline (development → pre-production → production → post-production → distribution) with no representation here.
  • Sensor height is currently a hardcoded constant (3:2 native assumption) in the interactive prototype rather than a real paired input alongside Sensor width.
  • Interactive prototype out of sync — the yaw/pitch/roll symbol convention was corrected in this document (§3.2) but not yet back-applied to content-space-3d-prototype-v7.jsx.
